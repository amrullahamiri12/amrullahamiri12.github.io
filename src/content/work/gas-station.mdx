## The problem

A gas station closes out once a day. The point-of-sale prints a Daily Close Report and
somebody has to prove it balances: fuel plus store plus tax minus discounts should equal
the grand total, the payment methods should add up to the same number a second way, and
the cash that actually went into the safe should match what the register says was owed.

I did this job. Before I wrote any of this I spent two years as an accountant owning
month-end close, and a daily close is the small version of that — the same reconciliation,
every day, per station, on paper. The failure mode is not that it is hard. It is that it
is repetitive, and repetitive work gets skimmed. A number typed into the wrong column in
July is found in October by somebody reading a bank statement.

Everything downstream depends on it. If the daily numbers are wrong, the month is wrong,
and the tax filing built on the month is wrong.

## What I built

Upload the day's PDF. The app pulls the values out, shows them next to the document they
came from, and refuses to approve the report until it balances. Corrections are recorded
with who made them. Only approved reports reach the dashboard, so nothing provisional ever
becomes a number somebody reports on.

Underneath it is a multi-tenant system: an operator role above companies, an admin inside
one, then area managers, station managers and viewers. Getting that boundary wrong would
show one company another company's revenue, so it is enforced at the data layer rather
than in the UI.

## The hardest part

**I decided the parser would not be a model.**

Extraction is rules — section detection, field matchers, value normalizers, and a mapper,
with separate parsers for the formats I actually see. It would have been faster to hand
the PDF to an LLM and ask for JSON.

The reason I didn't is that this is not a summarization problem. It is an arithmetic
problem with a right answer. A model that reads a Daily Close correctly 97% of the time
is wrong about three closes a quarter, and it is wrong *quietly* — a plausible number in
a plausible field. Somebody would find it in an audit. A rules parser that doesn't
recognise a format fails loudly instead, and the app falls back to manual entry.

The cost is real. Every new POS format is work — a new parser, not a new prompt. I
accepted that trade because in this domain a wrong number is worse than a missing one.

The same instinct runs through the money handling. Nothing is a float. Every amount is a
`Decimal` at 28 digits, rounding half-up, and the parser understands that `(1,234.56)`
means negative because that is what parentheses mean on an accounting statement.

What actually enforces the balance is a validation engine — a set of named rules,
each with a severity:

- `FUEL_GALLONS_X_PRICE` — gallons times price should equal fuel sales
- `FUEL_PLUS_STORE_VS_TOTAL` — the components should reconstruct the grand total
- `EXPECTED_VS_ACTUAL_CASH` and `CASH_OVER_SHORT` — what should be in the safe versus what is
- `CARWASH_DOUBLE_COUNT` — car wash counted once, not inside *and* beside store sales
- `DUPLICATE_DATE`, `MISSING_REPORT_DATES` — the days themselves have to be accounted for

Then the question that took longest: **how close is close enough?** Cash never ties to the
cent. Set the tolerance too tight and every report is a false alarm, and people learn to
click through warnings — which is worse than having none. Too loose and real errors pass.

I settled on a dollar for currency, a tenth of a gallon for volume, and 5% as a warning
threshold, all configurable per deployment, because that number is a business decision
wearing an engineering costume. It should not be a constant compiled into the app.

Overrides exist for the same reason. A manager who knows why a day is odd can approve it —
and the override is written down with their name on it.

## What I'd change

The domain model grew to 59 tables, which is honest to the problem — tanks, deliveries,
card settlement batches, tax mapping rules, monthly reconciliation — but I would introduce
the mapping-rule tables earlier rather than retrofitting them. Fifty-five migrations is
the record of learning that in public.

I would also add OCR. Right now a scanned PDF with no text layer goes to manual entry.
Extraction is modular specifically so OCR can drop in behind it, and I left that seam
deliberately — but it is still a gap.
