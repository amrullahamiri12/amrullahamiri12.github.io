## The problem

Small service businesses lose money in the gaps between their tools. A lead arrives by
email and nobody answers it for two days. An appointment is booked and the reminder is
never sent. An invoice goes out and the follow-up depends on somebody remembering. The
work that gets dropped is never the skilled work — it is the administrative connective
tissue around it.

These businesses already own the software. They have Gmail, a calendar, QuickBooks, maybe
a CRM. What they do not have is anyone to wire it together, and they are far too small to
hire someone who could.

## What I built

WiloSync connects the tools a company already uses and runs the repetitive work between
them: capturing and screening inbound leads, sending appointment and payment reminders,
requesting reviews after a job, and pulling the day's outstanding items into a single
digest. It reaches across roughly twenty integrations — Gmail, Slack, HubSpot, Google
Sheets, Calendly, QuickBooks, Stripe among them.

We aimed it at security and cleaning companies first. Both are appointment-based, both run
on thin margins, and in both a missed reminder is not an inconvenience — it is a crew
arriving somewhere nobody is expecting them.

Setup is not self-serve by default. A customer can buy a workflow build outright, and
pricing runs from a free single workflow up through tiers by workflow count. That is a
deliberate choice about who the customer is: the owner of a twelve-person cleaning company
does not want a canvas and a node editor. They want the thing to already work.

## The hardest part

**Screening a lead with a model means being wrong about a real person's enquiry.**

Lead screening is the piece customers care about most and the piece that carries the most
risk. A classifier that files a genuine enquiry as noise costs a business a job they never
learn they lost — a silent false negative, the worst kind, because nothing surfaces to tell
anyone the system failed.

That asymmetry is the thing I hold onto. Screening should rank and route, never discard —
the two error directions are not equally expensive, so the system should not treat them as
though they are. An owner who occasionally sees a junk enquiry keeps trusting the tool. An
owner who discovers it swallowed a real customer will not, and would be right not to.

The second hard part is that twenty integrations means twenty different failure modes.
Third-party APIs rate limit, expire tokens, change response shapes, and go down. A workflow
that a customer is depending on cannot fail silently when one of them does, which means
retries, token refresh, and making the difference between "not sent yet" and "will never
be sent" visible.

## What I'd change

The screening should be measured, not just built. Right now I can say it works; I cannot
put a number on how often it is wrong, or in which direction. That needs a labelled set of
real enquiries and an evaluation that runs on every change — which is the difference
between shipping a feature that uses a model and actually doing machine learning. It is
the next thing I want to get right.
