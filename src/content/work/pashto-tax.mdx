## The problem

Commercial language models bill by the token and measure their context window in
tokens. So the way a tokenizer cuts up your language is not a technical detail — it
decides what you pay and how much the model can hold at once.

Everyone in the field knows this penalises low-resource languages. I wanted the number
for mine.

I grew up speaking Pashto. Before I wrote software I spent years interpreting between
Pashto and English for hospitals, courts and 911 dispatchers. That is the position this
question comes from: not "which languages are underserved" in the abstract, but what it
costs the people I used to interpret for.

## What I built

A benchmark over FLORES-200 — a corpus professionally translated and sentence-aligned
across two hundred languages, which is what makes the comparison fair. Every language is
expressing the same 1,012 sentences, so a difference in token counts is a difference in
encoding rather than in content. Nine tokenizers, nine languages.

The result, on the same content:

|  | English | Pashto |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tokens (GPT-3.5 / GPT-4) | 27,182 | **102,084** |
| Cost per million words | $3.10 | **$9.66** |
| Sentences in a 128k context | 4,765 | **1,268** |

The clearest version is one word. In English *woman* is **one token**. In Pashto **ښځه**
— the same word, three characters — is **five**.

## The hardest part

The measurement was easy. The interesting work was the follow-up question: **why?**

The obvious answer is the writing system. Pashto uses the Perso-Arabic alphabet, and
byte-level tokenizers built mostly on English are known to handle non-Latin scripts
badly. If that were the whole story, Pashto, Persian, Urdu and Arabic would fare alike.

They don't — and BLOOM makes the reason legible. BLOOM was trained on 46 languages.
Urdu is one of them. Pashto is not.

To isolate it properly I split Pashto's own vocabulary in two: words containing the
letters Pashto does not share with its neighbours — ټ ډ ړ ږ ښ ګ ڼ ځ څ ۍ — and words
without them. If the script were the problem, both halves would cost the same.

| Tokenizer | Covers Pashto | Penalty on Pashto-only letters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BLOOM | no | **1.48×** |
| GPT-3.5 / GPT-4 | not by design | 1.23× |
| GPT-4o | not by design | 1.09× |
| XLM-RoBERTa | yes | **0.92×** — none at all |

The cost lands precisely on the letters a tokenizer never learns if no Pashto was in its
training data. It is not the alphabet. It is who was invited.

Two things surprised me. **GPT-4o's tokenizer cut Pashto by 55%** — 102,084 tokens down
to 46,064 for identical content. Whatever changed between `cl100k` and `o200k` was worth
real money to speakers of languages like mine. And **a bigger vocabulary is not
automatically a fairer one**: Mistral's 32k vocabulary is the worst here at 4.30×, but
BLOOM's 250k still charges Pashto 2.51× while XLM-RoBERTa's 250k charges 1.34×. What
matters is which languages the vocabulary was built to cover.

## What I would not claim

Token counts are not model quality. A tokenizer that fragments a language less is not
thereby better at understanding it, and I have tested nothing about comprehension,
generation or translation. The claim is narrow and economic: for the same sentence,
Pashto costs more tokens, and tokens are what you are billed for.

Fertility here is tokens per whitespace-delimited word — a blunt measure, and
deliberately the same blunt measure for every language. The ratios are computed on
identical content, so they don't depend on how words are counted.

FLORES covers Southern Pashto (`pbt_Arab`). Northern Pashto has orthographic
differences this doesn't capture.

## What's next

An evaluation set. Token counts say what the language costs; they say nothing about
what the models get *wrong*. The version of this I want to build next is Pashto–English
pairs from the domains I actually interpreted in — medical intake, court proceedings,
emergency dispatch — where a mistranslation has a consequence. That is a harder problem
and a more useful one, and building it is the part nobody can do for me.
